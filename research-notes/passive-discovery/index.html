Research note · October 2026

Reading together, without a keyword

Some connections between documents can be searched for. Others can't, because nobody could name the word. We try to find those with a cheap judge that asks one open question of a thousand pairs.

The documents here are one person's app log: 2,637 messages, searches, workouts, notes and assistant chats over 15 months. The person is user 002 of DynamicMem, a synthetic benchmark persona: a product designer in Amsterdam.

1The problem

Take one entry. Most other entries add nothing to it. A few, read together with it, tell you something that neither says alone. We want those.

2Some of these you can search for

The obvious move is to search with the anchor's own words. That works for part of it: of the 30 partners our method ends up ranking highest, 20 contain one of the words. But they sit in a pile of 222 matches, unranked. The other 10 share no word with the anchor at all, so no search finds them: a record-sorting puzzle, a photo walk to old windows, a run in autumn light.

3Our method

Cheap judges change what is affordable. The OpenAI Decisions API answers a yes/no question about a pair with a probability, at about seven cents per thousand pairs. So instead of naming a keyword, we name what we want and ask it of every pair: start from one entry, shape it into a question with a blank, fill the blank with 1,000 random entries, let the judge score each pair, and read only the top 30.

6What this shows


Both routes reached a good idea from the same starting entry, and they are different ideas. The agent searched by
keyword and found a fact no packet held: the camera is in "winter storage" while the same person keeps
"photographing" ice in software. It also saw 12 of the judge's 31 entries, the ink-well puzzle and the old window panes
among them. What its keywords never reached is the entry the judge ranked first: the April evening they cancel the AI
subscription to see that light "in actual physical space". That entry is what turns the others into a pattern.


On a log this small, grep is cheap and works well. The judge earns its keep where you cannot name what you are
looking for, or where the pile is too large to search by hand: it hands a reader 31 entries that already lean toward
the answer.
