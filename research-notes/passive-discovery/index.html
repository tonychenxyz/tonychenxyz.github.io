From search to discovery

Search finds what you already know to ask for. A cheap judge lets us ask every pair of documents an open question instead, and read only the pairs it picks.

Every square below is one action in one person's app log: 2,637 messages, searches, workouts, notes and assistant chats over 15 months, one block per month, coloured by app. The person is user 002 of DynamicMem, a synthetic benchmark persona: a product designer in Amsterdam. Hover any square to read it.

1Search is active

To search, you type what you are looking for. The log answers with exactly that.

2How active is a search?

The more precisely you ask, the more you already knew. An exact name returns the thing and nothing else. A loose word returns a pile you must read. The question we care about here, what about this person would surprise even them, has no words to type at all.

3What if we want more surprise?

Surprise lives in connections: two entries, months and apps apart, that mean something together and nothing alone. To find them without a keyword you would read pairs. One entry has 2,636 possible partners; the whole log has about 3.5 million pairs. Nobody reads that.

But a judge can. The OpenAI Decisions API answers a yes/no question about a pair with a probability, for about seven cents per thousand pairs. So we can ask a question too vague for any search engine, of every candidate pair, and read only what scores highest.

4Passive discovery

Start from one entry. Shape it into a question with a blank for a second entry. Fill the blank with 1,000 random entries, let the judge score each pair, and keep the top 30. Nothing in the question names what to find.

7What this shows


Both routes reached a good idea from the same starting entry, and they are different ideas. The agent searched by
keyword and found a fact no packet held: the camera is in "winter storage" while the same person keeps
"photographing" ice in software. It also saw 12 of the judge's 31 entries, the ink-well puzzle and the old window panes
among them. What its keywords never reached is the entry the judge ranked first: the April evening they cancel the AI
subscription to see that light "in actual physical space". That entry is what turns the others into a pattern.


On a log this small, grep is cheap and works well. Passive discovery earns its keep where you cannot name what you
are looking for, or where the pile is too large to search by hand: it hands a reader 31 entries that already lean
toward the surprise.
