Research note · October 2026

Reading together, without a keyword

Some connections between documents can be searched for. Others can't, because nobody could name the word. We find them with a cheap judge that asks one open question of a thousand pairs, and compare with an agent that searches.

1The data

One person's app log: 2,637 entries over 15 months, from 16 apps. Each square is one entry, one block per month. The person is user 002 of DynamicMem, a synthetic benchmark persona: a product designer in Amsterdam. Click an app to see where it sits and what an entry looks like.

2The task

Given one entry, find what in the rest of the log, read together with it, reveals something worth acting on. Here the something is a gift:

Task
Plan one surprise that will make this person happy. Build it on a non-obvious connection: entries from different apps or months that together reveal something about them that would surprise even them, which no single entry shows.

3What we found

We started from an evening chat on 6 November 2024 where the person asks an AI to render imaginary glass and ice. Our method returned 30 partner entries. Read together, they fall into three groups, each group says one thing, and the three combine into one idea. Click any dot or box to read what is behind it.

4How we did it

A keyword search can't do this: 10 of these 30 entries share no word with the anchor. Instead we name what we want and ask it of every pair. The OpenAI Decisions API answers a yes/no question about a pair with a probability, for about seven cents per thousand pairs, so we fill the question's blank with 1,000 random entries and read only the 30 that score highest.

8Memory for a kernel agent


The task is KernelBench-mega 02_kimi_linear_decode: one batch-1 decode step of a Kimi-Linear model (KDA,
MLA and an int4-weight MoE) written as a single kernel launch. Each agent is Codex (GPT-6.1 Sol) with one H100 and one
hour, scored by speedup over the benchmark's PyTorch baseline.


Before it starts, the agent can use notes left by earlier agents. The question is how it should look into them.

Two ways to use it



  
Files only

Our method

    • 
    
  • Score 5,000 notes on one question: would reading this next to that point suggest a way to make the kernel
      faster?
    •

Round 0 is three agents with no memory. In each later round, three fresh agents per arm start from

Not by keyword, once you search from the right place: the stuck agent's own words put it at rank 2 of 5,000 notes. The
difference is the starting point. Our method searched from where the agent was stuck; the files-only agents searched for
the names they had been given.

9The same question, on other corpora


In an app log, almost any two entries can be read together in some way. A harder test is a corpus where most
pairs are unrelated. We ran the same kind of pipeline, the Decisions judge scoring one open question for every pair, on
719 research-mathematics manuscripts (openai/math) and on
68,201 passages of medical evidence reviews (Cochrane). Three of the connections it surfaced are below, each
shown with the documents it was built from.



First, three research papers on C*-algebras, the mathematical objects used to describe quantum systems.



  
Two notions appear in all three. A quasitrace is a consistent way of assigning a size between
    0 and 1 to the parts of an algebra. An algebra is properly infinite if it contains two non-overlapping
    copies of itself.

  

    

      
A counterexample to Kaplansky's quasitrace conjecture

      
from the paper · theorem“A unital \(C^*\)-algebra \(C\)
        admits a normalized \(2\)-quasitrace if and only if \(M_m(C)\) is not properly infinite for every
        positive integer \(m\).”

      
In plain wordsThe paper disproves an old conjecture by constructing one
        counterexample. Along the way it quotes a known theorem: an algebra has a quasitrace exactly when none of its
        matrix versions \(M_m(C)\) (\(m\) copies arranged as an \(m\times m\) matrix) is properly infinite. So an algebra
        with no quasitrace has some \(m\) for which \(M_m(C)\) is properly infinite.

    

    

      
The Kirchberg–Rørdam character criterion

      
from the paper · theorem“Let \(A\) be a nonzero unital
        separable complex \(C^*\)-algebra, and let \(\omega\) be any free ultrafilter on \(\mathbb{N}\). Then
        \(F_\omega(A)\) has no characters \(\iff A \cong A \otimes_{\min} \mathcal{Z}\).”

      
In plain wordsThe paper gives a test for whether an algebra absorbs the
        Jiang–Su algebra \(\mathcal{Z}\), a regularity property that, among other things, lets its elements be cut
        into any number of nearly equal parts. The algebra passes exactly when an associated algebra, \(F_\omega(A)\), has
        no characters (no homomorphisms onto the complex numbers). It belongs to the research on divisibility.

    

    

      
Weak pure infiniteness and O-infinity absorption

      
from the paper · corollary“Let \(A\) be a separable nuclear
        complex \(C^*\)-algebra. If there is one integer \(n\ge 1\) such that \(h^{\oplus n}\) is properly infinite
        for every \(h\in A_+\), then \(A\cong A\otimes_{\min}\mathcal{O}_\infty\).”

      
In plain wordsThe paper asks how weak a form of infiniteness still implies
        the strongest one, absorbing \(\mathcal{O}_\infty\), which is the condition the classification theorems for
        infinite algebras require. Its answer: it is enough that, for one fixed \(n\), \(n\) copies of any positive
        element side by side are properly infinite. It belongs to the research on infiniteness.

    

  

  
Why the link is hard to seeThe three papers answer three different
    questions. The second is about divisibility and the third about infiniteness, two lines of research whose authors
    rarely cite each other. The statement that matters in the first paper is not its main result but a known theorem it
    quotes in passing. Papers are found by their main results, so a search on the first paper's topic would not return
    that statement.

  
read together

  
The conclusion

    
For these algebras, having no quasitrace already implies absorbing \(\mathcal{O}_\infty\), the
      strongest form of infiniteness.

    

      
paper 1No quasitrace implies that, for
        some \(m\), the matrix version \(M_m(A)\) is properly infinite.

      
paper 2No quasitrace also gives
        divisibility, which turns that statement about \(m\) copies into one about the algebra's own elements.

      
paper 3If \(n\) copies of every positive
        element are properly infinite, the algebra absorbs \(\mathcal{O}_\infty\).

      
togetherThe factor \(m\) drops out: no quasitrace ⇒ properly
        infinite after a fixed number of copies ⇒ absorbs \(\mathcal{O}_\infty\).

    

    
Why it's surprisingA purely negative condition, the absence of a
      quasitrace, implies the strongest positive form of infiniteness. And the step that makes it work comes from the
      divisibility results, which are not usually applied to infinite algebras.

    
Why it mattersIt would simplify a check. The classification theorems apply
      only to algebras that absorb \(\mathcal{O}_\infty\), and verifying that usually means constructing an isomorphism.
      This would let you verify it by showing that no quasitrace exists. It is a short combination of published results
      assembled by the reader, not a new theorem, and no specialist has checked it.

  





Next, two reviews of folic acid: one about malaria drugs, one about arsenic poisoning.



  

    

      
Published 2026

        
The review's page on the Cochrane Library.

        
Cochrane LibraryPubMed 41705996

      
from the review · background“The malaria parasite
        requires folate for survival and growth; antifolate antimalarial medications used for prevention and treatment
        target enzymes in folate metabolism as their mechanism of action. … Concerns have been raised
        about the potential effects of folic acid (including above the tolerable upper intake level (UL)
        >1.0 mg/day) in the context of malaria prevention and treatment, including the efficacy of antimalarial
        medications.”

      
In plain wordsMalaria parasites need folate (vitamin B9) to grow. Some
        malaria drugs, such as sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP), work by blocking the parasite's use of folate, so extra
        folic acid from supplements may weaken them. The review weighs that risk for folic acid given in pregnancy.

    

    

      
Published 2021

        
The review's page on the Cochrane Library.

        
Cochrane LibraryPubMed 34661903

      
from the review · conclusions“There is moderate-certainty
        evidence that FA supplements may benefit blood arsenic concentration, urinary arsenic methylation
        profiles, and plasma homocysteine concentration versus placebo.”
        same review · background“Arsenic is excreted
        following methylation reactions, which are mediated by folate.”

      
In plain wordsArsenic in drinking water is a widespread poison. The body
        gets rid of it through a chemical step that uses folate, so folic acid (FA) supplements are being tested as a way
        to help people excrete it. Trials show they lower the arsenic in people's blood.

    

  

  
The connection is not obviousThe two reviews share more than it first
    appears: both come from the folate research groups at Cornell and the US CDC, and both have folic acid in the title,
    so a search for folic acid would put them side by side. What neither does is connect them. The 2026 malaria review,
    written five years after the arsenic review by the same institutions, discusses folic acid given in pregnancy, not
    folic acid given against arsenic; the arsenic review does not mention malaria drugs. The judge ranked the arsenic
    review 62nd of 10,000 partners for the malaria review.

  
read together

  
The conclusion

    
Giving folic acid to protect people from arsenic could weaken an important malaria drug in places
      that have both problems.

    

      
malaria reviewExtra folic acid can weaken
        malaria drugs that work by blocking folate, such as SP.

      
arsenic reviewFolic acid is being
        tested to help people excrete arsenic.

      
togetherWhere arsenic-contaminated water and SP-based malaria
        prevention occur together, a folic-acid programme against arsenic could reduce the protection SP gives: two
        public-health measures working against each other.

    

    
What is known and what is notThe interference between folic acid and SP is
      well documented: several randomised trials found more SP treatment failures when folic acid was added, most clearly
      at doses above 1 mg a day. That folic acid lowers blood arsenic is also established. We found no paper connecting
      the two, in the reader's searches or ours. The arsenic trials used 400 to 800 µg a day, below the level where
      the interference with SP is clearest, so how large any effect would be is uncertain.

    
Why it's surprisingTwo programmes that each do good could undercut each
      other. And the 2026 review of exactly this drug interaction, from the same institutions that reviewed folic acid
      for arsenic, does not raise it.

    
Why it mattersArsenic in groundwater and SP-based malaria prevention overlap
      in some regions; northern Burkina Faso, for example, has documented arsenic in its wells and gives young children
      SP-based seasonal malaria chemoprevention. Anyone planning folic acid against arsenic there would need to know. It
      can be checked directly: compare SP's protection in people with and without folic acid supplements in such a
      region, or schedule the folic acid away from SP doses, as is already advised in pregnancy.

  





Finally, two reviews on preventing urinary tract infections, where the link is a contradiction between their numbers.



  

    

      
Published 2023 · fifth update since 1998

        
Cranberries for preventing urinary tract infections

        
Cochrane LibraryPubMed 37947276

      
from the review · main results“In moderate certainty evidence,
        cranberry products reduced the risk of UTIs (6211 participants: RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.84; I² = 69%).
        … Compared to probiotics, cranberry products may reduce the risk of symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs
        (3 studies, 215 participants: RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.56; I = 0%).”

      
In plain wordsThe review pools 50 trials of cranberry juice and tablets.
        Compared with placebo or no treatment, cranberry lowers the risk of a urinary infection to 0.70 times, about 30%
        less. Three small trials compared cranberry with probiotics directly, and there cranberry lowered the risk to 0.39
        times that of probiotics, about 60% less.

    

    

      
Published 2015

        
Probiotics for preventing urinary tract infections in adults and children

        
Cochrane LibraryPubMed 26695595

      
from the review · main results“We found no significant
        reduction in the risk of recurrent symptomatic bacterial UTI between patients treated with probiotics and placebo
        (6 studies, 352 participants: RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.12; I(2) = 23%) with wide confidence
        intervals…”

      
In plain wordsThe review pools small trials of probiotics (beneficial
        bacteria) taken to prevent urinary infections. Compared with placebo, probiotics lower the risk to 0.82 times or
        not at all; the evidence is weak, but nothing in it suggests that probiotics increase infections.

      
The
        judge paired the cranberry results with this review's objectives section, which asks whether probiotics beat
        placebo or cranberry juice. The result quoted above is from the same review's results section.

    

  

  
The connection is not obviousBoth reviews are about preventing urinary
    infections, but they were written by different teams eight years apart, and each reports its own comparisons.
    Neither number looks wrong on its own. The problem appears only when you combine the cranberry review's two
    comparisons into an implied third one and set it next to the probiotics review's direct measurement. Reviews are not
    usually read that way, and neither review cites the other's figure.

  
read together

  
The conclusion

    
The cranberry review's result that cranberry beats probiotics contradicts the probiotics review, so it
      is probably overstated.

    

      
cranberry reviewCranberry lowers the risk to
        0.70 times placebo, and to 0.39 times probiotics.

      
probiotics reviewProbiotics measured
        directly against placebo: 0.82, with no sign of any increase.

      
togetherDividing the cranberry numbers gives an implied
        probiotics-versus-placebo ratio of 0.70 / 0.39 ≈ 1.80, an 80% increase in risk. The measured value is 0.82.
        The two differ by a factor of about 2.2, roughly three standard errors, so the three small trials behind the 0.39
        most likely overstate cranberry's advantage.

    

    
Why it's surprisingTaken at face value, a result in a current Cochrane review
      implies that probiotics cause urinary infections, which no trial has found. Neither review shows this on its own: it
      takes one number from each.

    
What it is and is notThis is a check on a published estimate, not a new
      medical finding. It says nothing new about cranberries or probiotics themselves. Part of the gap could come from
      different patients: the cranberry estimate pools children, women with recurrent infections and people with
      catheters, while the probiotics trials mostly enrolled women with recurrent infections. We have not checked whether
      anyone has published this inconsistency.

    
Why it mattersAnyone choosing between cranberry and probiotics on the strength
      of the cranberry review, and any guidance that ranks cranberry above probiotics on that basis, relies on an estimate
      that conflicts with other evidence. It can be checked directly: a network meta-analysis with cranberry, probiotic
      and placebo arms would test whether the direct and indirect evidence agree.

  





In each example the link is written in none of the documents: a chain of three published results, a vitamin shared by
two unrelated programmes, a contradiction between two reported numbers, and in section 3 a shared material. A keyword search can bring the documents up, but it
cannot surface a link that none of them states.
