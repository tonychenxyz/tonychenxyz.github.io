Reading together, without a keyword

Some connections between documents can be searched for. Others can't, because nobody could name the word. We find them with a cheap judge that asks one open question of a thousand pairs, and compare with an agent that searches.

1The data

One person's app log: 2,637 entries over 15 months, from 16 apps. Each square is one entry, one block per month. The person is user 002 of DynamicMem, a synthetic benchmark persona: a product designer in Amsterdam. Click an app to see where it sits and what an entry looks like.

2The task

Given one entry, find what in the rest of the log, read together with it, reveals something worth acting on. Here the something is a gift:

Task
Plan one surprise that will make this person happy. Build it on a non-obvious connection: entries from different apps or months that together reveal something about them that would surprise even them, which no single entry shows.

3What we found

We started from an evening chat on 6 November 2024 where the person asks an AI to render imaginary glass and ice. Our method returned 30 partner entries. Read together, they fall into three groups, each group says one thing, and the three combine into one idea. Click any dot or box to read what is behind it.

4How we did it

A keyword search can't do this: 10 of these 30 entries share no word with the anchor. Instead we name what we want and ask it of every pair. The OpenAI Decisions API answers a yes/no question about a pair with a probability, for about seven cents per thousand pairs, so we fill the question's blank with 1,000 random entries and read only the 30 that score highest.

8The same question, on mathematics


An app log is forgiving: almost any two entries can be read together somehow. So we ran the identical setup on a
corpus where that isn't true — 719 research-mathematics manuscripts across 17 fields
(openai/math). Same judge, same single open question, 10,000 random
partners per anchor, and the top 100 handed to one reader that also had the whole corpus on disk and web search, so it
could check whether anything it found was already known.


Five anchors, picked at random. Four came back with nothing — which is the honest answer when a paper has
no real neighbour in the corpus, and those four reports are mostly a list of candidates the reader killed itself. The
fifth found something, out of three documents that look nothing alike.



The first is a counterexample paper: here is a strange object that breaks a conjecture from the 1960s.
You would file it under curiosities. The sentence that mattered isn't even its result — it's a classical fact the
paper recites in passing: a system admits no notion of size at all exactly when it can be split into two copies of
itself, provided you first bundle m copies of it together.


The second is about when a system is infinitely divisible — sand rather than bricks. It says: if a certain
derived object casts no "one‑dimensional shadow", the system is sand.


The third is about the opposite situation: when a system contains two complete copies of itself, like
Hilbert's hotel. It says bundled splitting already forces the strongest form of this.





What the three give together
A system is maximally infinite exactly when you
cannot measure it — not even sloppily. Having no notion of size, the weakest imaginable failure, already forces
the strongest form of self‑duplication.



The surprise is that the m vanishes. Needing to bundle m copies before you can split something looks
strictly weaker than splitting it outright — you needed extra room to do the trick. But being unmeasurable forces
the system to be sand, and once it is sand you can divide the inequality through by m. The weaker‑looking
condition was never weaker.



Why a specialist wouldn't stumble on this: the second and third papers sit on opposite sides of the field's main
dividing line, the one between finite and infinite behaviour, and the argument runs the unexpected way — it uses
a finite‑world tool to settle an infinite‑world question. Two of the three are used for something that isn't
their point, and people index papers by their headline. The three describe one phenomenon in three different dialects
— "no measure", "no shadow", "swallows a factor" — which sound like three unrelated ideas. The step that
actually closes the argument is a fourth, published fact that none of them mentions. And all three appeared within 48
hours of each other inside a drop of 719 papers, so nobody had read them side by side.



What it is worth, stated plainly: this is a cleaner, more checkable form of a known gateway condition, not a new
theorem. "Split this in two" asks you to build something; "no measure exists" asks you to rule something out, which is
usually far easier. Two of the three inputs carry machine‑checked Lean proofs; the third does not. And once you
grant the middle paper, the remaining step is short enough that a specialist may already have it. The reader said so
itself, and rated its own find medium rather than high.
