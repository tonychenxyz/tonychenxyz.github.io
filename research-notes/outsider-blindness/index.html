LLM’s Outsider Blindness

What frontier AI doesn’t know about how the world works

By Tony Chen
Explore all 500 questions Insider-500 on Hugging Face
Contents

AI keeps getting better at well-specified tasks. But we still can’t drop it into the open world and ask it to “make me a billion dollars” or “propose impactful research.”

People say “taste matters,” and in practice domain experts still work alongside AI to get real problems solved. So where exactly is the gap?

Insider-500: Evaluating practitioner knowledge with podcast Q&A

Why podcasts?

Most of what practitioners know never gets written down, and what does goes through editors, lawyers and PR first.

Long interviews draw out more. Experts skip most decision steps when they describe their own work until an interviewer prompts them (Sullivan et al., 2014). Relaxed guests also say more than they meant to: one court weighed a CEO’s podcast remarks over his own trial testimony (In re Mindbody, 2023).

In long podcast interviews, people who do the work explain how it actually works, with details they rarely write down.

We took interviews with 100 practitioners in 20 industries, from bankers and chip designers to chefs and physicists. From each, we picked five moments where the guest explains how something works, wrote each as a plain question without naming the guest, and split the answer into three or four rubric points. The result is Insider-500: 500 questions, each with a clip of a practitioner answering it.

Frontier models miss more than half of the rubric points

How we kept models from finding the answers

Every model used the same search tool, with its built-in web tools turned off. The tool dropped results from 122 domains (video and audio hosts, transcript sites and each podcast’s own site) and from all 100 source videos by ID.

In a rerun that also refused any search naming the guest, scores changed by at most 0.2 points.

We asked six frontier models from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI every question, with answers capped at about 180 words. GPT-6.2-Sol graded each answer one rubric point at a time, quoting the sentence that makes it. Any wording counts.

The models also miss the same things. 43% of the rubric points were missed by all six, even though they come from three labs that train them separately. Picking the best of the six answers to every question would still reach only 55%.

Every model could search the web, and it barely mattered. On the questions where a model chose to search, search added between 0.6 and 3.9 points, and none of these gains is statistically significant. Here is what the searching looked like.

  • They search for the literature. Asked how management responds to a short seller, Carbon planned to look for “empirical findings from studies like Brendel and Ryans,” and found them. The studies describe what companies disclose. Carson Block describes the earnings calendar.
  • They search for what they already believe. On Gavin Baker’s question, Carbon’s second query was “fragmentation AI chip startups helps Nvidia software moat.” That became its answer.
  • They search with generic words from the question. More than half of Gemini 4 Neon’s queries take most of their words from the question. What comes back is news, papers, filings and Wikipedia: the field described from outside.

Have the models gotten better?

We ran five generations of Claude Opus, from 4.6 to 5.5, on a fixed set of 100 questions, five from each industry.

Yes, but slowly.

What are the models missing?

The models’ answers are reasonable. They read like what a smart person would write after reading everything published about a field without ever working in it. We call this outsider blindness. The misses fall into a few patterns, all about how things work.

Some of the models’ answers are also true: software compatibility does make chip rewrites hard, and cheap steel does help shipyards. A rubric built from one person’s answer isn’t the only right answer. But the practitioners are rarely adding one more item to a list. They say which factor decides the outcome, and often name the common explanation and set it aside, as Nima does. That judgment is what we set out to measure.

Do the models know it somewhere deep inside?

Maybe the models know these things but don’t say them, and reinforcement learning could sharpen them out. We haven’t run RL, but if the knowledge is in the model, it should show up across many samples. We sampled eight answers to 50 questions from Gemini 4 Neon and GPT-6.2-Sol, with and without web search.

  • 74 of the 168 rubric points never appeared in any of Neon’s eight answers, and 97 never appeared in Sol’s.
  • About 40% of Sol’s best-of-eight gain was grader noise. It went away when we graded the same answers a second time.

For most rubric points, the answer is no: sampling doesn’t reach them. Nor is the length limit to blame, since eight answers run past a thousand words.

How did the practitioners learn these things?

Not from reading. In their answers, they often say where it came from.

They learned by doing the work and talking to the people involved, and little of that gets written down. What does get said in public is outnumbered by text written from the outside, which is what pretraining and search see. The scores by industry fit this picture.

Fields that publish their reasoning, in court opinions and papers, come out on top. Fields where most of what people know stays in the work come out at the bottom.

Our numbers likely understate the gap. Everything we tested was said in public, and most of what practitioners know never reaches a microphone.

How do we close the gap?

  • Better priors. Put more of how the world actually works into pretraining and midtraining, so a model starts out closer to the practitioner.
    • Paying experts to write it down runs into incentives. For the best practitioners, this knowledge is what makes them valuable, and an hourly rate is little reason to hand it over.
    • Much of it is local and goes out of date fast.
  • Learning from people. Let models learn it the way practitioners do, inside real environments.
    • People compress years of local experience into a few sentences, as these podcasts show.
    • A model has to know what to ask the loan officer or the shipyard manager, and notice when the answer contradicts what it believed.
    • That also takes infrastructure for talking with people in real time.

How we built and graded the test

Questions. 100 practitioners, five per industry in 20 industries, each from one long-form interview on YouTube. We transcribed the interviews with ElevenLabs and lightly cleaned them of filler words. For each person we took five passages where they explain how something works, and wrote a question a curious user might ask, without naming the person or asking the model to play a role. We avoided “what should I do” questions. Each answer was split into three or four short rubric points, and each rubric point has a grader note saying what counts and what doesn’t.

Models. Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Neon in Antigravity, GPT-6.2-Sol and GPT-6.2-Astra in Codex. Each was asked for a plain-prose answer under 180 words. The Claude models often went over (median 185 and 195 words); cutting their answers at 180 words and grading again moves Fable from 44.3% to 43.4% and Opus from 42.7% to 41.5%.

Search. All models used the same search tool, with their built-in web tools turned off. It blocked 122 domains (video and audio hosts, transcript sites and the podcasts’ own sites) and all 100 source video IDs. A rerun that refused any search naming the guest changed scores by at most 0.2 points.

Grading. GPT-6.2-Sol graded every answer against the rubric points and grader notes, and had to quote the sentence that made each rubric point; quotes were checked against the answer. Grading 1,344 verdicts a second time gave 96.6% agreement (κ = 0.92). In the sampling test, Neon’s answers were graded by GPT-6.1-Sol and Sol’s by GPT-6.2-Sol; with and without search use the same grader. With a second grading, Sol’s best-of-eight gain falls from 8.9 to 5.3 points.

Limits. Each question has one practitioner’s answer, and other experts might weigh things differently. The grader accepts any wording, but it can still miss a rubric point made indirectly. Many interviews were public before the models were trained, so the models may have seen them. If anything, that makes the test easier.
