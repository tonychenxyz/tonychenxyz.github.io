LLM’s Outsider Blindness

What frontier AI doesn’t know about how the world works

AI labs have a concrete picture of where this goes. You train a model as good as your best researcher, then run thousands of copies of it. Each copy picks its own problems and decides what to try next. Dario Amodei calls this “a country of geniuses in a datacenter.”

We are not there yet. Models already write much of the code at AI labs, but researchers still decide what the code is for. Outside AI it is the same. A model can do a well-specified task in almost any field. Give it an open-ended job, like choosing which research bet to make or fixing a broken supply chain, and you still want someone from the field checking its work.

Ask people why, and you hear “taste matters” or “you still need domain experts.” I think they are right, but that doesn’t tell us what to fix. What does the expert know that the model doesn’t?

The test

To find out, we turned to podcasts. In a long interview, people who do the work explain how it actually works, in their own words, with details they rarely write down. We picked interviews with 100 practitioners in 20 industries: bankers, chip designers, short sellers, chefs, airline executives, physicists and AI researchers. From each interview we took five moments where the guest explains how something works, and wrote each one as a plain question anyone might ask an AI, without naming the guest. That gave us 500 questions. We asked six frontier models from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI to answer them.

Why podcasts?

Most practitioners never write down how their work actually runs. What does get written, like papers, filings and press releases, passes through editors, lawyers and PR first. A long interview is different in two ways.

First, the host keeps asking. Experts leave out much of what they know when they describe their own work, usually without noticing. In one small study, surgeons teaching a procedure left out 73% of the decision steps. When an interviewer prompted them, the share of steps they described rose from 44% to 66% (Sullivan et al., 2014).

Second, people relax. Over a long conversation, guests drop their prepared lines and say what they actually think, sometimes more than they meant to. After Mindbody was sold, its CEO told a podcast host that 98% of his net worth had been locked in the company’s stock, and that selling it bit by bit was “kind of like sucking through a very small straw.” When shareholders later sued over the sale, the Delaware Court of Chancery quoted that line and found his own statements outside the courtroom “far more persuasive than the trial testimony” (In re Mindbody, 2023).

Podcasts are also public and timestamped, so every answer here can be checked against the original clip. Each example on this page has a button that plays it.

Here is one of the questions: why does nobody at a frontier AI lab know the true capability ceiling of a model it has just released?

All six models gave the same kind of answer. Capability isn’t a fixed property, it depends on prompts, tools and scaffolding, and evaluations can only show a lower bound. That is all true. Noam Brown of OpenAI gave a different reason on the No Priors podcast: to know what a model can do after a month of work, you have to run it for a month, and new models come out every two or three months. As models take on longer tasks, finding their limits takes longer, and the next model ships first. None of the six models said this, including the two made by OpenAI.

The other two tabs, about bank lending and commodity stocks, follow the same pattern. The questions are plain, and the models’ answers are reasonable. They read like what a smart person would write after reading everything published about a field without ever working in it. The practitioners answer from inside. We call this gap outsider blindness.

To measure it, we split each practitioner’s answer into three or four points, 1,691 in all. GPT-6.2-Sol graded every answer, and for each point it either quoted the sentence that makes it or marked it missing. An answer gets a point if it says the same thing in any words. Answers were limited to about 180 words, and every model could search the web with the source podcasts, video sites and transcript sites blocked.

Here every model made the textbook point that people like structured answers. Four made the point about labs training on each other’s outputs. None made Schulman’s most concrete point: that the people hired to write training data paste in answers from their favorite chatbot.

What they miss

Across all 500 questions, the best models made about 44% of the points. The GPT-6.2 models made about 30%, even though a GPT-6.2 model was the grader.

Why do the GPT-6.2 models score lower?

Their answers are shorter (a median of about 135 words, against 157 to 195 for the others) and more hedged: words like “may”, “can” and “depends” come up about three times as often per word. They make fewer separate claims, and each claim is another chance to make one of the practitioner’s points. Even at the same length, 145 to 175 words, they score lower: 25.3% and 26.3%, against 40.6% to 48.0% for the others.

The gap between labs isn’t the main result here. The points that all six models miss are.

The scores turned out to matter less than which points the models missed. For each of the 1,691 points, count how many of the six models made it.

723 points, 43%, were missed by all six models. Another 328 were made by all six. The models come from three labs that train them separately, and they miss largely the same things. If you could pick the best of the six answers to every question, you would still get only 55%. On 50 questions, no model made a single point.

A fair objection is that several of the models’ answers are also true. Software compatibility does make chip rewrites hard, and cheap steel does help shipyards. A rubric built from one person’s answer can’t be the only right answer. But the practitioners are rarely adding one more item to a list. They are saying which factor decides the outcome, and often they name the common explanation and set it aside, as Nima does. That judgment, about which factor matters most, is what we set out to measure.

Is it in there somewhere?

Maybe the models know these things and just don’t say them. Post-training pushes models toward safe, standard answers, and the practitioner’s view could still be sitting in the tail of the distribution. If so, reinforcement learning could sharpen the model and bring it out.

We haven’t run RL on this, but we can check the premise. If the knowledge is in the model, it should show up when we sample many answers. We took 50 of the questions, with 168 points, and sampled eight answers to each from Gemini Neon and GPT-6.2-Sol.

Of the 168 points, Neon made 56 in all eight answers and 74 in none. GPT-6.2-Sol never made 97 of them. Taking the best of eight answers raises Neon’s score from 45.5% to 55.5%, and part of that gain is grader noise: when we graded Sol’s answers a second time, about 40% of its best-of-eight gain went away.

So for most of these points, the answer is no. The knowledge doesn’t seem to be in the model in a form that sampling can reach. This also means the length limit isn’t what holds the models back. Eight answers come to over a thousand words, and within each model, longer answers don’t score higher.

Is it on the web?

Every answer in this test was said out loud on a podcast, and podcasts are part of the public internet. So in some sense the information is out there. Could the models find it by searching?

All the runs above allowed web search, with the source episodes blocked so the models couldn’t copy the answers. To see what search added, we ran every model again with search turned off. No model moved by more than a point (Figure 4). Gemini Carbon searched 577 times across 205 questions and gained 0.7 points. Claude Fable 5.2 searched once in 500 questions and still had the top score. With eight samples, Neon reached 55.5% with search and 55.3% without.

Carson Block’s question from above shows what happens when models do search. Four of them searched 17 times between them and found an academic study of how companies respond to short sellers. None of them made any of Block’s four points.

Search hands the model more of what it has already read: papers, news and commentary written about a field from the outside.

Where did the practitioners learn it?

Look at how the practitioners came to know these things. Jigar Shah tried to borrow against a $500 million pipeline and heard the loan officers’ reasons. Carson Block has watched the earnings of the companies he shorted, one quarter after another. Jim Keller has sat between chip architects and the marketing team. Noam Brown builds the models and sees how long it takes to find their limits. Nima Arkani-Hamed has spent years at blackboards with mathematicians.

None of them learned it by reading about their field. They learned it by doing the work and talking to the people involved. Very little of that gets written down. What does get said, on a podcast for example, sits on an internet where most of the text about any field is written from outside: textbooks, news, analyst notes, blog posts and summaries. Pretraining sees the world in that proportion, and so does search.

The scores by industry fit this picture.

Models do best in law, biotech, robotics and science, where much of the reasoning is published in court opinions and papers. They do worst in fintech, manufacturing, construction, food and investing, where most of what people know stays in the work.

It also suggests our numbers understate the gap. Everything we tested was said in public. Most of what practitioners know is never said into a microphone at all.

Closing the gap

If outsider blindness comes from where models learn, there are two ways to close it.

The first is a better prior. Put more of how the world actually works into pretraining and midtraining, so a model starts out closer to the practitioner. The obvious way is to pay experts to write it down, and that runs into a problem. For the best practitioners, this knowledge is what makes them valuable, and an hourly rate is little reason to hand it over. Much of it is also local and goes out of date fast. Getting it into training data will take better incentives than paying for labels.

The second is to let models learn it the way practitioners do: inside real environments, and from people. People are very good at compressing years of local experience into a few sentences, as these podcasts show. A model that knows what to ask the loan officer or the shipyard manager, and notices when the answer contradicts what it believed, can pick up much of this on the job. That needs models that are good at talking with people, and the infrastructure to let them do it in real time.

How we built and graded the test

Questions. 100 practitioners, five per industry in 20 industries, each from one long-form interview on YouTube. We transcribed the interviews with ElevenLabs and lightly cleaned them of filler words. For each person we took five passages where they explain how something works, and wrote a question a curious user might ask, without naming the person or asking the model to play a role. We avoided “what should I do” questions. Each answer was split into three or four short points (1,691 in total), and each point has a grader note saying what counts and what doesn’t.

Models. Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, Gemini Carbon and Gemini Neon in Antigravity, GPT-6.2-Sol and GPT-6.2-Astra in Codex. Each was asked for a plain-prose answer under 180 words. The Claude models often went over (median 185 and 195 words); cutting their answers at 180 words and grading again moves Fable from 44.3% to 43.4% and Opus from 42.7% to 41.5%.

Search. All models used the same search tool, with their built-in web tools turned off. It blocked 122 domains (video and audio hosts, transcript sites and the podcasts’ own sites) and all 100 source video IDs. A rerun that refused any search naming the guest changed scores by at most 0.2 points.

Grading. GPT-6.2-Sol graded every answer against the points and grader notes, and had to quote the sentence that made each point; quotes were checked against the answer. Grading 1,344 verdicts a second time gave 96.6% agreement (κ = 0.92). In the sampling test, Neon’s answers were graded by GPT-6.1-Sol and Sol’s by GPT-6.2-Sol; with and without search use the same grader. With a second grading, Sol’s best-of-eight gain falls from 8.9 to 5.3 points.

Limits. Each question has one practitioner’s answer, and other experts might weigh things differently. The grader accepts any wording, but it can still miss a point made indirectly. Many interviews were public before the models were trained, so the models may have seen them. If anything, that makes the test easier.
