Read together

Good ideas often come from reading unrelated things side by side. We built a graph that links text worth reading together, and it finds connections that similarity search misses.

  • Idea: link pieces of text that spark a new thought when read together, not pieces that look alike.
  • Method: ask a cheap model one yes/no question about a small random sample of pairs, and infer the rest from chains.
  • Result: on a week of earnings calls, 78% of JEV's top 100 edges hold a real insight, against 28% for embedding similarity and 29% for TF-IDF.

1. The idea

Insight often sits between documents, not inside them. Darwin read Malthus on population growth; neither text contains natural selection, but reading them together did. In a big pile of text such combinations are easy to miss, because the pieces rarely share a topic or a keyword.

The usual tools link text for other reasons: where it is filed, or how alike it is. None of them captures this.

2. How we build it

We use JEV, a model from TypeSafe that reads text but only answers multiple-choice questions, which makes it cheap. For each pair we ask:

“Would a reader who reads excerpt A and excerpt B together have new thoughts that they wouldn't have from either one alone?”

The catch is the number of pairs. N paragraphs make N×(N−1)/2 pairs, so the count grows with the square of the pile. One busy week of earnings calls has 8,693 paragraphs and 37.8 million pairs. At about $0.00002 per pair, asking JEV about every pair would cost about $830 for that week, about $19,000 for a quarter (41,303 paragraphs, 853 million pairs), and roughly $300,000 for a year.

So we build the graph approximately. We don't need JEV's answer for every pair, only a way to tell which paragraphs go together: if A goes with C, and C goes with B, then A and B are probably worth reading together too. So we ask about a small random sample of pairs (JEV edges) and estimate the rest from chains (inferred edges):

When to stop. As the graph fills in, the average gap on new pairs shrinks. We stop once it stops shrinking (the last five rounds are no more than 5% better than the five before), or when the budget runs out. On the real week the gap leveled off after $2.36, with JEV edges covering just 0.28% of all pairs:

What would a general-purpose model cost? JEV costs $0.042 per million tokens it reads. GPT-5.6-Luna, one of the cheapest general models, costs $0.20 per million tokens read and $1.20 per million written (OpenAI pricing). Asking Luna the same questions, batched the same way and answered with one word per pair, would cost:

  • Our run (107,510 pairs): about $11, instead of $2.36.
  • Every pair of the week (37.8 million): about $3,850, instead of about $830.

That is roughly 4.6 times more, and it assumes Luna does no step-by-step reasoning, which would be billed as extra output. Luna also answers in words, so it would not directly give the probability of “yes” that JEV uses as an edge's weight.

3. JEV versus similarity search

We took the JEV edges between different companies (107,214 pairs) and ranked them three ways: by JEV's weight; by embedding similarity (Qwen3-Embedding-4B), which matches meaning; and by TF-IDF, which matches words, counting rare words more. A separate model, GPT-5.6-Luna, then scored each method's top 100 pairs from 1 to 5: does reading the two together give a specific, non-obvious insight? (Luna's question is close to JEV's own, so this test leans in JEV's favour.)

Here are the top five pairs of each list. Both similarity methods rank call sign-offs first, since every call ends with the same polite words. JEV ranks first pairs that share a real issue.
