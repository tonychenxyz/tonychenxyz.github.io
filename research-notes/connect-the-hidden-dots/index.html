Research note, October 2026

Connect the Hidden Dots

Seeing that two unrelated-looking things belong together is a big part of what we call insight. Today's models may be smart enough for it, but their harnesses only let them search, and you can only search for what you expect. We built a way to bring them the pieces without a query.

A coastline and a fossil

In 1910 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener, looking at a world map, noticed something any schoolchild could see, and set it aside as improbable. A year later he happened on a report about fossils that had nothing to do with maps, and the two together changed geology.

The map

The coasts of South America and Africa have matching shapes.

+
The fossil

Mesosaurus, a small reptile of lakes and shallow water, is found in Brazil and in southern Africa. It could not swim an ocean.

=
Together

The continents were once joined, and moved apart.

Neither piece was new: the map was in every atlas, and paleontologists already knew the fossils, which they explained with a sunken land bridge. What Wegener added was reading the two together, and he found the second one by accident.

Why agents miss it

Wegener's fossil report, the essay on population that Darwin read, a utility's remark about who turned up at its substation: in each case the second piece came from somewhere nobody would have thought to look. Connecting it takes real intelligence, and today's models may well have enough: hand an agent both pieces and it often sees the link, as in the four examples above. The gap is in the harness. An agent only reads what its tools bring back, and those tools are built for active search.

Keyword search and embedding search both start from a query, and a query is a guess about what you will find. Search for “Meta capex” and you get more about Meta and capital spending; the PG&E call shares neither the words nor the topic, so it never comes up. A surprise is exactly the thing you didn't know to search for.

We see the same thing in our own experiments: agents given the same documents and a search tool walk past the links our method finds. The kernel section below has an example.

Wegener came across his fossil report “quite accidentally.” Our method makes that kind of accident systematic.

How it works

The method starts from one document, pairs it with thousands of others, asks a cheap model one question about each pair, and keeps the pairs that score highest.

Does it help?

In each test the same agents get different evidence, and we compare what they do with it. The evidence is chosen in one of three ways:

Random documents from the same pile.
Full search: the agent searches the whole pile itself.
Our method, with one, two or three hops.

Betting on the news

On four dates between August 15 and October 1, 2026, each agent (GPT-5.6 Sol) got $10,000 to bet on any open Polymarket question and held every bet until it settled. It read 50 news articles chosen by its method, or searched about 1 GB of news itself, and 75 agents ran on each date.

Nothing from after a decision date can reach an agent. An article is kept only if Common Crawl captured it at least an hour before the decision time, market rules come from archived copies made by then, and prices are the last ones observed before it. The agents have no web access, only the files we give them, and the model's stated knowledge cutoff, February 2026, comes before every date.

Mean return per agent:

Two hops was the only method that made money on average (+8.9%), and it beat random on all four dates, while every other method lost money, random the most (-24.0%).

Trading on earnings calls

On four dates between August 1 and September 15, 2026, each agent built a portfolio of 6 to 20 equal-sized long or short positions and held it for two weeks. It read 60 earnings-call excerpts chosen by its method, or all the calls, and 60 agents ran per method.

Every excerpt comes from a call held before the date, the agents have no tools beyond the text they are given, and the model's knowledge cutoff comes before every date. The green line is the S&P 500 (SPY) bought and held over the same windows.

No method beat the index on return. On Sharpe, full search (2.10) roughly matched it (2.12), one hop came next (1.46), and two hops trailed (0.86).

Picking proteins

Green fluorescent protein has a published brightness measurement for a pool of 500 variants. Each agent reads from 3,486 papers about GFP and picks the 10 variants it expects to be brightest, with 9 agents per method. How often did a pick land in the true top ten of the 500? Each square is one pick, and filled squares are hits.

Hits are rare for every method, close to the 1.8 in 90 that random picks would give, and all but one are the same variant, N105H. No method found better proteins here.

Mathematics: a chain through three papersopenai/math

The corpus is openai/math, 719 manuscripts an OpenAI model wrote on open research problems. These three are about C*-algebras, the objects used to describe quantum systems. Two ideas appear in all three. A quasitrace is a consistent way of giving each part of an algebra a size between 0 and 1. An algebra is properly infinite if it contains two non-overlapping copies of itself. Papers are found by their main results; the first paper's key line is a theorem it quotes in passing, so a search on its topic would not return it.

Writing GPU kernels

On a KernelBench decode-kernel task, each agent (GPT-6.1 Sol) gets one H100 and one hour, and its score is how many times faster its kernel runs than the PyTorch reference. Over three rounds, three agents per method learn from 33,192 notes on what earlier agents tried. Either the agent searches the notes itself with grep, or our method takes the points where the previous run got stuck, scores notes against each, and hands over the best ones with a one-page brief.

The two fastest kernels, at 34.0× and 36.3×, both came from our method, and both use a trick none of the other 19 kernels uses: DP4A, which multiplies 8-bit integers four at a time instead of converting the 4-bit weights to bf16.

The note behind it

“W4A8 dp4a integration passes correctness in the fused decode kernel.” It records a middle step in an earlier run, so it doesn't look like a solution.

Why search missed it

The grep agents looked for finished solutions and read the ends of runs. The two that searched for “dp4a” pointed grep at the wrong file.

Why our method found it

Read next to the stuck point, the note turns out to share its problem: both were limited by how fast the weights stream.

The full story, with every note, is in the earlier note.

What's next

01
Scan the whole pile

Run the pairing across a whole corpus at once. Do the best links get even less expected?

02
Let agents choose

Most groups are noise. Can an agent sort them and keep only the ones worth reading?

03
Better groups

Score the pairs inside each group as well, to find the tightest groups.
