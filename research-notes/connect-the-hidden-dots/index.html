Research note, October 2026

Connect the Hidden Dots

Seeing that two unrelated-looking things belong together is a big part of what we call insight. Today's agents are bad at it, because they find things by searching, and you can only search for what you expect. We built a method that finds the links without a query.

A coastline and a fossil

In 1910 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener looked at a world map and noticed something any schoolchild could see. He set it aside. A year later he read a report on fossils that had nothing to do with maps.

The map

The coasts of South America and Africa have matching shapes.

+
The fossil

Mesosaurus, a small reptile of lakes and shallow water, is found in Brazil and in southern Africa. It could not swim an ocean.

=
Together

The continents were once joined, and moved apart.

South America turns about the pole of the 1965 Bullard fit; the two ● fossil sites meet.

Neither piece was new. The map was in every atlas, and the paleontologists already had the fossils. They explained them with a sunken land bridge. Wegener read the two together and reached a different conclusion. He found the second piece by accident.

Why agents can't do it yet

Wegener, Darwin reading Malthus, the analyst who hears about a substation tour: in each case the second piece came from somewhere nobody would have looked. Connecting it is a real kind of intelligence. Agents today mostly don't have it, and the reason is how they get information. They search.

Keyword search and embedding search both start from a query, and a query is a guess about what you'll find. Search “Meta capex” and you get more about Meta and capex. The PG&E call shares none of those words and none of that topic, so it never comes up. A surprise is, by definition, the thing you didn't know to search for.

We see this whenever we give an agent the same documents and a search tool:

0 of 9

agents with grep over 33,192 kernel notes opened the note behind the two fastest kernels. They searched for finished solutions, and two who typed “dp4a” pointed grep at the wrong file. Kernels

Wegener didn't search for the fossil report. He came across it “quite accidentally.” Our method makes coming across things systematic.

How it works

Start from one document. Pair it with many others, ask a cheap model one question about each pair, and keep the pairs it likes.

Does it help?

We gave the same agents different evidence and compared what they did with it. Three ways of choosing evidence keep coming back:

Random documents from the same pile.
Full search: the agent searches the whole pile itself.
Our method, with one, two or three hops.

Each reader got 50 articles chosen by its method, or search over all of them. Mean return per reader:

Two hops was the only method that made money on average, +8.9%, and it beat random on all four dates. Everything else lost money, random worst at -24.0%. Read this with care:

  • Four dates is a small sample, and one date (Sep 1) carries most of the gain.
  • With 100 articles per reader instead of 50, in an earlier run, every method lost money, two hops included (-3.2%).
  • Prices are historical and fees are ignored. The model that scores pairs is newer than the dates, so its choices could carry knowledge of what happened.

Mathematics: a chain through three papersopenai/math

The corpus is openai/math, 719 manuscripts an OpenAI model wrote on open research problems. These three are about C*-algebras, the objects used to describe quantum systems. Two ideas appear in all three. A quasitrace is a consistent way of giving each part of an algebra a size between 0 and 1. An algebra is properly infinite if it contains two non-overlapping copies of itself. Papers are found by their main results; the first paper's key line is a theorem it quotes in passing, so a search on its topic would not return it.

For these algebras, having no quasitrace already implies absorbing O∞, the strongest form of infiniteness.

Why it's surprising: a purely negative condition implies the strongest positive form of infiniteness, and the step that makes it work comes from divisibility results not usually applied to infinite algebras. It is a short combination of published results assembled by the reader, not a new theorem, and no specialist has checked it.

Nothing here has been verified as new. “Nobody seems to have asked this” is a much weaker claim than “this is new.”

Writing GPU kernels

KernelBenchone decode kernel task, one H100, one hour
33,192 notes on what earlier agents tried
3 roundseach learning from the last
3 agents per method per round, GPT-6.1 Sol

Score is how many times faster the agent's kernel runs than the PyTorch reference. Either the agent searches the notes itself with grep, or our method takes the points where the previous run got stuck, scores notes against each, and hands over the best ones with a one-page brief.

The two fastest kernels, 34.0× and 36.3×, came from our method. Both use the same trick, DP4A: multiply 8-bit integers four at a time instead of converting the 4-bit weights to bf16. None of the other 19 kernels uses it.

The note behind it

“W4A8 dp4a integration passes correctness in the fused decode kernel.” A middle step in an earlier run, not a final answer.

Why search missed it

The grep agents looked for finished solutions and read the ends of runs. The two that searched for “dp4a” pointed grep at the wrong file.

Why our method found it

Read next to the stuck point, the note shares the problem, not the answer: both were limited by how fast weights stream.

Three agents per method per round is a small sample, and round 2 went the other way. The full story, with every note, is in the earlier note.

What's next

01
Scan the whole pile

Run the pairing across a full corpus, not one starting point at a time. Do the best links get even less expected?

02
Let agents choose

Most groups are noise. Can an agent sort them and keep only the ones worth reading?

03
Better groups

Score pairs inside a group too, not only against the starting document.

Forecasting. Polymarket questions and rules from the Wayback Machine; news text from CC-NEWS, the same fixed set for every method. Markets covered: 48–76% of those open on each date. Readers: GPT-5.6 Sol (high). Pair scores: OpenAI Decisions API, gpt-6-luna. 299 portfolios; one random reader failed and is left out. Returns use the historical price nearest the decision time, no fees; with a 5¢ cost per contract every method loses money, two hops least (-5.0%).

Trading. 240 portfolios, idealized fills at the daily average price, no costs. 17 of 2,507 positions had no price and count as zero. Sharpe is the mean over readers of each portfolio's annualized ratio of mean to standard deviation of daily returns, with no risk-free rate; the S&P 500 line is $10,000 of SPY scored by the same code over the same windows. Transcripts were downloaded in October 2026, not as they read at the time.

Proteins. ProteinGym GFP assay, a fixed pool of 500 variants. The 30- and 60-paper studies used different readers and packets.

News pages. Article screenshots were taken on October 26, 2026 from the live pages and link to them; the readers saw the CC-NEWS text, not these pages.
