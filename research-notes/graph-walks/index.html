Walking the read-together graph

We gave AI agents a graph to walk over a big pile of text. Its edges link passages that are worth reading together. Does walking it surface connections the agent wouldn't find by searching? We tested it on three corpora: earnings calls, one person's app history, and the notes from 823 earlier GPU-kernel coding runs.

A walk. The agent expands a snippet, a judge scores random partners, and it steps along the strongest links.

In short

Not proven. The graph changed where agents looked. On earnings, two of the three graph-walking arms did better than the files-only agent and one did worse. The graph also pulled walks toward a few hub topics.

  • Earnings calls (24 agents, 6 rounds). Over all six rounds, Luna was the only arm with positive alpha (+2.4% annualized, Sharpe 0.40). JEV came second (−13%), ahead of files only (−21%). Decisions came last (−30%). The first three rounds looked better for the graph (Luna +9%). The next three were worse for every arm, and none of the alphas is statistically significant.
  • The walk did find the evidence. 57–65% of the calls the graph agents cited were first reached through a judged edge. The cross-company pairs JEV cited share about 28% less vocabulary than the files-only agent's, which it found with keyword search. Luna's and Decisions' pairs share only slightly less.
  • Personal memory (9 agents). The ideas concentrated. Three of the nine surprises were a hand-made glass object. The other six were a game night, a fireside dinner or a sauna restart: the same four threads that earlier runs kept finding. The JEV and Decisions graphs rate the user's "glass render" entries high against almost everything, so walks drain into them.
  • KernelBench (a 3-hour GPU task). No clear help. With the graph as its only way into memory, the Luna agent reached 45.6×, close to the files-only agent's 46.1×. The JEV agent reached 34.6×, below an agent with no memory at all.
  • Cost and speed. Judging 5,000 pairs cost about 10¢ with JEV and 26–31¢ with Luna or Decisions. JEV judged them in 10–15 seconds, Decisions in 17–36 seconds and Luna in 2–2.5 minutes, leaving out a few Decisions calls that stalled. The exploring agent itself costs 10–100× more than any judge.

1How a walk works

Each corpus is cut into snippets: a paragraph of an earnings call, one entry in someone's app log, one note from an earlier coding run. Two snippets get an edge when a judge model thinks they are worth reading together, because together they say something neither says alone. Snippets from the same company are never paired: links within a company are the obvious ones.

Nobody judges every pair in advance. The earnings corpus alone has 140,316 snippets, or about 10 billion pairs. So the agent grows the graph as it walks, with four commands:

  • random draws snippets at random, optionally within a date range.
  • view shows a snippet, the previous and next snippet of its document, and every partner judged so far, strongest first.
  • expand id K makes sure the snippet has K judged partners. Missing ones are random snippets from other companies, scored by the judge. This is the only way new edges appear.
  • log lists what the agent has seen, so it can cite exact ids at the end.

Try it on a toy corpus of 24 made-up snippets from six industries. Expand a snippet, then click a partner to walk there. Strong links tend to cross industries.

In the real runs each agent had a budget of 5,000 judged pairs (the sum of every expand's K) and had to spend all of it. The prompt said that an edge is noisy, and that a snippet should be judged by its top several partners together, never by one edge. Every run started from a fresh, empty edge cache, so no agent could follow another agent's trail. All agents in a round started from the same random snippet.

Three judges, and a baseline with no graph

The judge decides which pairs become edges. We tried three. The baseline agent gets the same model, the same time and the same corpus, but as plain files with search tools and no graph.

The exploring agents ran in sealed sandboxes, with no internet, no view of the repository and no way to see the other agents. On earnings they were GPT-5.6 Sol at high reasoning through codex exec, with 30 minutes each. On personal memory they were Claude Opus 5.5, with 20 minutes each. On KernelBench they were Claude Opus 5.5, with 3 hours and their own H100.

2Earnings calls: a long-short portfolio

The corpus is every earnings call that WRDS (S&P Capital IQ) has from April to June 2026: the last full quarter before GPT-5.6 Sol came out on July 9. Each agent is a portfolio manager on July 1, 2026. It builds an equal-weight long-short portfolio from what the calls say, and picks its own buy and sell dates for each stock between July 1 and October 8. The prompt asks for non-consensus insight: a supplier and its customers telling different stories, a second-order effect on another industry, a contradiction between peers.

Returns

Six rounds ran in two batches of three, each round from its own random start. The chart combines each arm's six portfolios with equal money; the chips show single rounds. Over the same window the S&P 500 (SPY) returned +4.5%. These portfolios hold longs and shorts, so they barely move with the market (beta 0.19–0.37 pooled).

The ranking changed with more rounds. After three rounds, all three graph arms led files only. Rounds 4–6 were weaker for every arm, and Decisions lost the most (−8%, −4% and −15%). Luna stayed ahead because of a few big winners: Lumentum (+39%), AXT (+45%) and PBF (+38%) longs. One trade decided a lot everywhere. Shorting Nike, whose quarter was flattered by a one-time tariff refund, made money all 12 times it was picked. Shorting HP lost money all 9 times (−20% to −49% each). Some positions in every arm had no usable prices, so all four results are partial sums.

The replays show a pattern we didn't design for. The agents spent the whole 5,000-pair budget in the first 1.3 to 6 minutes, in 5 to 25 huge expansions of 200–1,000 partners each. They then spent the rest of the 30 minutes reading those partner lists, interleaved with many filtered random draws. So the walk was wide, not deep: few steps from the start, each looking at hundreds of candidates.

Where the cited evidence came from

For each call an agent cited, we traced how the agent first got into that call. Was it listed as a judged partner, or drawn at random? The graph supplied most of the evidence. This is the most direct sign that walking the graph mattered.

What kind of connections came up

Every position below cites two or more companies. Badges show how the agent reached each snippet. The graph arms' connections tend to cross industries. The files-only agent's tend to follow a keyword through one industry: memory prices, tanker rates, athletic apparel.

  • JEV (31 of 66 positions cite more than one company) built commodity and logistics chains: EU fertilizer duties and Fertiglobe, lost Gulf cargoes lengthening LPG voyages, gas pipelines for behind-the-meter AI power (Welspun, with Emerson). It also chained memory costs into PC makers (HP) and an Italian IoT maker, and fertilizer into wheat and a bread maker (Flowers Foods).
  • Luna (34 of 66) kept returning to "physical bottlenecks": a pump-laser shortage (Lumentum, with TE Connectivity), indium-phosphide wafers behind optical parts (AXT, with Celestica), tanker spot rates (MISC, read through an Indian refiner), caustic soda (Olin, through Westlake's outages) and refining (PBF). Its shorts were mostly companies absorbing those costs.
  • Decisions (23 of 57) went along AI-infrastructure chains: optical networks (Ciena with Credo), Caterpillar's standby engines turning into data-center prime power, Seagate's nearline drives allocated through 2027. It also shorted AI-exposed service firms (Concentrix, Upwork, ASGN). The ASGN short lost 75%, its worst trade, and its Lavoro short had delisted from Nasdaq in February.
  • Files only (27 of 66) searched for words like "shortage", "allocation" and "backlog", 140–210 times per run. Its links follow a keyword through a theme: memory pricing (SanDisk, Netgear, indie, Corsair), clean tankers, and GLP-1 coverage and GoodRx.

Were the connections less obvious?

"Non-obvious" is hard to score, so we use two rough proxies. The first is shared vocabulary: for every pair of cited snippets from different companies in the same position, the TF-IDF cosine of their words. Keyword search finds pairs that share words. A judge can link pairs that don't.

The second proxy is crowding: how often the other 23 portfolios picked the same name. Here the graph helped a little. Names nobody else picked made up 59% of the JEV and Luna positions, 56% of the Decisions positions and 53% of the files-only positions. But every arm crowded into the same few trades: shorting Nike (12 of 24 portfolios), shorting HP (9), buying Micron (7) and shorting Lululemon (6).

3Personal memory: plan a surprise

This corpus is the app history of one synthetic person from the DynamicMem benchmark (user 002). Mira is a product designer in Amsterdam. Their history has 2,637 entries from 16 apps, October 2023 to December 2024: WhatsApp, Maps, bank transactions, chats with an AI assistant, Notion, Spotify, Fitbit and more. The task: plan one surprise for early January 2025 that would make them happy, and explain it. Three graph agents (JEV, Luna and Decisions) each had 20 minutes and 5,000 judged pairs, over three rounds. This batch had no files-only agent; earlier rounds did.

The ideas concentrate

Nine agents, four kinds of idea. The same few threads come back across every setup we ran, including the files-only agents. The table includes the eight earlier runs, which used a shared edge cache, and in runs 1–4 gave the graph agents the files too. Hover over a theme to highlight it.

Three things concentrated the ideas.

  • The log has a few loud, unfinished threads. Mira renders the same glass block with AI all year. They ask Lars for aged cheese before every game night. They paused their sauna "for the summer" and never went back. They search for fireside bistros every Friday. Any careful reader finds these. Most graph agents also read nearly the whole log through view loops: five of the nine opened 1,800 or more of the 2,637 entries.
  • The judges have hubs. In the JEV and Decisions graphs the glass-render entries score high against almost anything, so they rise to the top of unrelated partner lists. A walk that follows top partners drains into them. Luna's graph shows this much more weakly (chart below).
  • A shared cache spread trails between runs. With one shared edge cache, run 5's JEV agent found the glass thread. Its edges then put glass entries at the top of later runs' start snippets, and every JEV agent from run 5 to run 8 proposed glass (4 of 4). With a fresh cache per run, JEV proposed glass once in three runs and Decisions twice.

4KernelBench: speeding up a GPU kernel with memory

Here the corpus is 33,248 notes from 823 earlier agent runs on KernelBench. Each note records one step of a run: what the agent tried, what happened and how its score moved. The task is open-ended engineering. In 3 hours on an H100, make one fused kernel for a Kimi-Linear decode step as fast as possible, scored as a speed-up over a PyTorch baseline. The public H100 record was 24.3×. On our faster H100 that record's code scores about 27×, and every agent with memory access starts by copying it.

In the graph-only run, the JEV and Luna agents had no memory files at all, only the corpus command with a 2,000-pair budget. Both started from the same random note, about a different kernel (a W4A16 matrix multiply on another GPU). The other runs used the memory as files, with or without the graph, or had no memory. The other runs had somewhat different prompts and are single runs, so read this as one rough comparison.

A walk adds little when the task names one problem. Both graph-only agents mostly read notes about the target kernel itself: 80 of the 102 notes the Luna agent read, and 131 of 133 for the JEV agent. Luna's other 21 notes came from two other problems. Both agents saw notes describing a 4-bit-weight, 8-bit-activation trick from an earlier run. Luna built its kernel around that trick and JEV didn't, a difference in what the agents chose to build, not in what the graph showed them. A grep over the files reaches a named problem's notes directly. The best score came from the files-only agent.

5Cost and speed

The judges differ a lot in speed, and less in price. The fastest is JEV, then Decisions, with Luna about ten times slower than JEV. The race below replays how long each judge took to score an agent's 5,000 pairs on earnings, at 20× speed. JEV scores up to 50 partners in one call that takes 0.6–1.2 seconds. Decisions scores 20 per call in 1–2.5 seconds, and Luna 10 per call in 5–7 seconds. Every call can run in parallel, but an expansion waits for its slowest call.

In practice the judge is cheap next to the exploring agent. A 20-minute Claude Opus 5.5 explorer cost $2–14 at API list prices, against $0.10–0.30 for its judge. A 30-minute GPT-5.6 Sol explorer read 37–62 million input tokens (98% from cache). The judge mostly costs time: Luna's 2–2.5 minutes of judging is 7–8% of a 30-minute run, and JEV's 10–15 seconds is under 1%.

6Does walking the graph reveal less obvious connections?

Partly, on earnings. Evidence for it: the graph supplied most of the cited evidence, JEV's cross-company pairs share less vocabulary than keyword search finds, the graph arms picked slightly more names nobody else did, and Luna and JEV beat files only on alpha and Sharpe over six rounds. Evidence against it: Decisions did worst of all, the first three rounds' lead shrank with three more, no alpha is significant, and every arm crowded into the same Nike and HP shorts.

Not on personal memory. There the graph narrowed the search. Judge hubs and, before fresh caches, other agents' trails pulled walks toward the same glass thread. The other ideas match what a careful reader of the whole log finds anyway.

Not on KernelBench, where the target is named and search reaches it directly.

What we would change next:

  • Correct for hubs. Rank each snippet's partners by how unusual a score is for that partner, or keep only mutual top-k links, so one popular thread can't top every list.
  • Make walks deep, not just wide. Every agent spent its budget in the first few minutes on huge expansions. Smaller budgets per step, or a cap on K, would force multi-hop paths. Multi-hop paths are where non-obvious chains should come from.
  • Score non-obviousness directly. Have blind raters judge whether each cited connection is surprising and holds up, and run many more rounds, so differences of a few points can be told apart from luck.

Setup details. Earnings: Codex GPT-5.6 Sol (high), October 8, 2026, six rounds in two batches of three, run in parallel over two accounts. Returns use Yahoo adjusted closes through October 7 in USD, equal weight per position, no trading or borrow costs. Positions with no usable prices keep their weight and count as zero. Alpha and Sharpe use daily excess returns against SPY and the 13-week T-bill. The files-only agent of round 4 cited calls only by abbreviated ids, so its citations are not in the evidence charts. Personal memory: Claude Opus 5.5 (high), three rounds run in parallel, October 8. KernelBench: Claude Opus 5.5, 3 hours on an H100 80GB HBM3, regraded on a clean copy (median of three benchmark runs).

Judges. JEV is TypeSafe's decision model (jev-1.13). Its edge weight is its yes-probability. Luna is gpt-6-luna with reasoning off through the OpenAI Responses API, writing a probability per partner. Decisions is the OpenAI Decisions API (gpt-6-luna), asked one predicate per partner. "Glass-thread entries" are those whose text mentions glass, permafrost, ice blocks, caustics, refraction, optics, prisms, the user's #0052FF blue or "20mm". That is 14% of the log.

Lexical overlap is TF-IDF cosine (sublinear tf, English stop words), fitted on 6,000 random snippets plus the cited ones. Example quotes are cut from the cited snippets. Theses are the agents' own words.
