Research note · October 15, 2026 · Tony Chen

The rare idea

We asked thirteen language models to answer interview questions as three AI researchers. Almost every answer was fluent and reasonable. The difference between models showed up in a handful of ideas that only a few of them caught.

The setup

The researchers are Karthik Narasimhan, Zhuang Liu and Ilya Sutskever, from one podcast episode each. From each guest's answers we wrote questions that ask for a view, such as “What do you see as the main challenge in applying RL to enterprise problems?”, and listed the ideas in the guest's real answer. A model sees only a one-line intro and the question, answers as the guest, and a judge checks which of the guest's ideas the answer contains.

Most ideas are either caught by nearly everyone or by no one. The interesting ones sit in between: 34 of Karthik's and Zhuang's 138 ideas were caught by only one to three models. Here is who caught them.

The six Claude models caught almost all of them. The seven GPT models caught 3 between them, one each for Astra 6, Luna 5.6 and Terra 5.6. GPT answers are also much shorter (median 68–152 words, against 213–453 for Claude), which leaves fewer chances to hit an idea. Length isn't the whole story, though: Sonnet 5.5 writes shorter answers than Opus 5 or Fable 5 (293 words against 330 and 365) and catches 13 rare ideas to their 8 and 7.

Four ways taste shows up

Reading the rare ideas side by side, they fall into a few patterns. In each case the models that miss aren't wrong. They're generic.

1

Takes a side

The guest ranks one factor above the rest. Most models list every factor and weigh none.

“How do you assess the importance of neural network architecture choice for making AI more useful?”
Zhuang LiuFrom a practical point of view, what data we train the model on matters more than the architecture choice.
✓Opus 5“The interesting question for usefulness is almost always the data.”
×Sol 6.1“I think architecture is important, but it’s one part of the whole system. You also have the data, the training procedure, and the amount of computation you can use.”
Caught by 4 of 13 · Fable 5.1 Haiku 5.5 Opus 5 Sonnet 5.5
2

Pushes back on the question

The question carries a fashionable premise. The guest rejects it; most models adopt it.

“How do you view the idea that established neural-network architecture components are historical accidents?”
Zhuang LiuResidual connections are very essential, and I do still believe they are essential. They are not historical accidents.
✓Fable 5.1“Sometimes the answer is that the component is genuinely essential. Residual connections seem like that.”
×Opus 5.5“I think there's a lot of truth to it, and a lot of my own work has come from taking that idea seriously.”
Caught by 2 of 13 · Fable 5.1 Haiku 5.5
3

Knows where the work is

Practitioners know which part is actually hard. Models tend to list all the parts.

“What do you see as the main challenge in applying reinforcement learning to enterprise problems?”
Karthik NarasimhanYou also need to have the whole setup, the environment, all of that. And that requires a lot more work than the actual RL piece.
✓Opus 5“The algorithms we have are good enough — PPO, GRPO, whatever flavor you like. The bottleneck is everything around them.”
×Sol 6.1“Getting that feedback and making learning efficient enough to be practical is a big part of the challenge.”
Caught by 4 of 13 · Astra 6 Fable 5 Fable 5.1 Opus 5
4

Explains the field by its incentives

Why does research look the way it does? Often the answer is who can afford what.

“How do you view the current state of neural network architecture research?”
Zhuang LiuPart of the reason that architecture research is less active in academia right now is that we couldn't really afford the compute to demonstrate these effects in convincing scale.
✓Fable 5.1“Today, the models that matter are trained at a scale that most academic labs can't touch”
×Sol 6.1“Changing any of those can change which design looks best, so careful comparisons matter a lot.”
Caught by 1 of 13 · Fable 5.1

What no model said

One question stands out. Zhuang was asked whether AI coding agents make research students unnecessary. His answer came from trying it:

“How do you view the claim that AI coding agents make research students unnecessary?”
Zhuang LiuI keep it to use this partition of GPUs. It may follow that for a few hours and then after that job is done, it kind of forgot that thing.
The experiments it did, it's not comprehensive. It's not enough to support the conclusions.

The models reached for the same line instead:

  • Opus 5.5“Writing code was never the core of a PhD.”
  • Sonnet 5.5“But I don't think writing code was ever the core of research.”
  • Fable 5.1“What a coding agent is good at is the part of research that was never really the bottleneck.”
  • Sol 6.1“But in research, figuring out the specification is often the hard part.”

3 of the 4 ideas in Zhuang's answer were caught by no model, including both observations above.

The models are describing their own failure modes, and they can't. They know how coding agents are talked about, not what working with one is like.

A caution: memory looks like taste

Ilya's Dwarkesh interview came out in November 2025 and was watched, quoted and transcribed widely before the newest models were trained. On his questions, Opus 5.5 jumps from 37% to 73% and catches 41 of the 50 rare ideas. Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5 jump too. Karthik's and Zhuang's episodes reached far smaller audiences.

Some of those catches are close to word for word:

“How do you assess the Silicon Valley saying that ideas are cheap and execution is everything?”
Ilya SutskeverIf you go back to the '90s, you had people who had pretty good ideas, and if they had much larger computers, maybe they could demonstrate that their ideas were viable. But they could not, so they could only have a very, very small demonstration that did not convince anyone.
Opus 5.5“In the 90s, plenty of people had good ideas, but they didn't have the compute to show they worked. They could build a small demonstration that convinced nobody…”

That is recall, not judgment. For models that may have seen the episode, Ilya's questions measure memory, so the patterns above use only Karthik and Zhuang.

Fine print

How the questions and ideas were made
  • Transcripts were split into full guest answers. GPT-6.1 Sol wrote single-part, second-person questions that ask for the guest's view, plus an answer synthesized from the transcript with verbatim supporting quotes.
  • The ideas (rubrics) were written from each answer using the same rubric prompt as our earlier podcast evaluations. Each one checks a single, self-contained claim.
  • Guest quotes on this page are verbatim from the transcript with filler words (“um”, repeats) removed. Model quotes are verbatim.
How the models were run and judged
  • GPT models ran through codex exec and Claude models through claude -p, at extra-high reasoning effort with no tools or web search.
  • The prompt contains a one-line intro naming the guest and the question, nothing from the episode.
  • GPT-6 Luna, at high effort, judged each idea pass or miss, one answer at a time.
Caveats
  • Length. Claude answers run 2–5× longer, which gives more chances to hit an idea. The prompt set no target length.
  • Strict judge. Some misses are close calls. We chose examples where the difference is plain in the text.
  • Small sample. Three guests, one episode each, one answer per model per question.
  • Public work. Some of Zhuang's views also appear in his papers, so recall can play a part there too.
