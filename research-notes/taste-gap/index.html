Do models understand the world the way experts do?

Open-ended work like research is hard to grade, both by its results and by its individual decisions. We argue for testing the understanding behind the decisions instead, and compare 13 models with three AI researchers.

Why decisions are hard to grade

AI is starting to take on open-ended work, like doing research or running a business. In this work, it has to make important decisions in a complex world.

The end result of this work is hard to check. It can take years to know whether a research direction or a business plan was right, and luck plays a large role.

One alternative is to check the steps: test whether a model makes the same decisions an expert made along the way. This has four problems.

  1. Each decision is hard to set up. You have to decide what information the person had and what their options were. That choice controls how hard the test is. For example, in 2021 Zhuang Liu decided to test whether ConvNets had really fallen behind vision transformers, or whether the gap came from small design details. If you tell a model about those design details, the answer is easy. If you don’t, the model has to guess what Zhuang knew.
  2. Decisions happen over many steps. People act, see what happens, and act again. Many small early steps set up the final decision, which may be obvious by the time it comes. People take steps in different orders, and at most points there are several reasonable next steps, so there is no single right answer. Many decisions also depend on things that happened long ago or were never recorded. This makes a realistic test environment very hard to build.
  3. Important decisions are rare. Most of what people do day to day is routine.
  4. People don’t like being recorded. But many are happy to talk about their work, for example on podcasts.

Test understanding instead

Behind all of these decisions is the person’s understanding of the world: what is true, what matters, and what is hard. This understanding drives the important decisions, and it is often what separates experts with good taste from others. Unlike decisions, understanding can be tested directly, with clear standalone questions that need no environment.

This is also how people explain their decisions. When you ask someone what to do, they often start by describing how the world works.

So we tested this. From one podcast interview each with Karthik Narasimhan, Zhuang Liu and Ilya Sutskever, we wrote standalone questions about how they see their field, and listed the ideas in their actual answers. We had 13 models answer each question as the guest. A judge model checked which of the guest’s ideas each answer included. The answers were fluent and reasonable. When they missed the expert’s idea, they tended to fail in one of five ways.

Talking around the question

The model lists what to consider instead of giving an answer. “I think the important question is…”

How do you judge when improvements on an AI benchmark are worth pursuing?
Karthik Narasimhan

If you're at 20% and then you go up to 80%, that's a very clear signal. But if everyone's at 80% and you're climbing to 81 and 82, at that point it's probably worth your time to actually look at the next thing.

The idea: Once models score near the top of a benchmark, small gains mean little.
×Sol 6.1“I think the first question is: what are we trying to learn from this benchmark?”
×Luna 6“I’d ask whether the improvement tells us something useful about what the system can actually do.”
✓Opus 5.5*“If it's at 90-plus, you're often fitting noise, label errors, or quirks of the test set, and the marginal gain tells you little about the underlying capability.”
✓Fable 5.1*“If a benchmark is at 95 percent, the last few points are almost always noise or label errors.”

The textbook answer

The model gives the standard explanation and misses the practical reason the expert gives. “The biggest challenge is the reward signal.”

What do you see as the main challenge in applying reinforcement learning to enterprise problems?
Karthik Narasimhan

You want to be using RL for the right things, but you also need to have the whole setup, the environment, all of that. And that requires a lot more work than the actual RL piece.

The idea: Building the setup around RL, like the environment and the reward, takes more work than the RL itself.
×Sol 6.1“I think the biggest challenge is that reinforcement learning typically needs a lot of trial and error, and in an enterprise setting, that trial and error has a real cost.”
×Luna 6“The main challenge is that enterprise problems rarely come with a clean, immediate reward signal.”
✓Opus 5“Honestly, it's not the learning algorithm. That's the thing people get wrong. The algorithms we have are good enough — PPO, GRPO, whatever flavor you like. The bottleneck is everything around them.”
✓Astra 6“So you need both a safe way to gather experience and a feedback signal that reflects the actual business objective. Getting those pieces right is often harder than choosing the reinforcement learning algorithm.”

Zhuang Liu

Not everybody is able to work on the fundamental side on these very large models. People who can afford to train them can experiment with that. That's why we see a lot of agents work right now.

Why do you think improving AI agents’ memory is more important than building collaborative agents?
Zhuang Liu

Even if you have unlimited context, in theory you can access everything you told the model, but if it forgets…

The fact that we need a lot of agents is exactly because one agent cannot remember everything.

The idea: Even with unlimited context, an agent can still forget or get facts wrong.
×Opus 5.5“Look at how agents work today. You give one a task, it does something reasonable for twenty minutes or an hour, and then the context fills up, or the session ends, and almost everything it learned is gone.”
×Opus 5“If you look at why people shard a task across five agents, very often it's because one agent can't hold the whole problem in its head — the context runs out, or it loses track of what it already tried.”
